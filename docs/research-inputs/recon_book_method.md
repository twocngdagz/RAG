# Study-Technique Inventory — Barbara Oakley, *A Mind for Numbers*

All descriptions below are paraphrased summaries of the book's advice and stated reasoning, written for use as product design input. Chapter numbers are approximate (based on the 18-chapter structure).

**Category legend:** `study-time practice` · `scheduling-and-habit` · `memory-encoding` · `metacognitive check` · `test-day tactic`

---

## 1. Alternating focused and diffuse thinking

- **Mechanism.** The book's central model: the brain has two broadly incompatible thinking states. One is narrow, deliberate, sequential attention aimed at material already partly familiar; the other is a relaxed, wandering, wide-ranging state that connects distant ideas. You cannot occupy both at once. New material typically resists the first state, because the relevant connections don't exist yet — so progress requires deliberately releasing attention so the loose state can operate in the background.
- **Learner procedure.** Grapple hard with the new concept or problem first, with full attention and no distractions. At the point of frustration or stall, deliberately stop and do something that requires no concentration — walk, shower, exercise, chores, sleep, a different subject. Return later. Repeat over several days rather than compressing into one session.
- **Category.** study-time practice (with a scheduling consequence).
- **Chapter.** 1–3, revisited in 12.
- **Domain.** General. The book illustrates it with maths and physics because symbolic material is where the "stuck" experience is most acute, but it explicitly notes the same alternation applies to writing, music, and any creative work. Equivalents: for PTE/IELTS, wrestle with a hard writing prompt or a confusing listening passage, then step away before revising; for interview prep, draft an answer to a hard behavioural question, leave it overnight, redraft; for primary literacy, short daily reading bursts with play between, not one long session.

---

## 2. Chunking — how a chunk is formed

- **Mechanism.** A chunk is a set of pieces of information bound into one retrievable unit by meaning, supported by a connected neural pattern. Its function is working-memory economy: working memory holds only about four items, so compressing a procedure or concept into one unit frees slots for new material. Isolated memorised facts do not chunk — the book's image is a puzzle piece with no interlocking edges.
- **Learner procedure.** Three stated steps: (a) give the material undistracted attention — background TV or message-checking prevents chunk formation; (b) understand the underlying idea, not just the steps, since understanding is what binds the traces together; (c) acquire context by practising with varied related *and* unrelated problems, so you learn not only how to apply the chunk but when it applies and when it does not. Critically, the book warns that the moment of understanding is not itself a chunk — you must close the book and reproduce the work unaided, and review soon after first exposure or the trace fades.
- **Category.** study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 4, expanded in 7.
- **Domain.** General in principle, illustrated with problem types. Equivalents: a grammar pattern or verb conjugation you can deploy without reconstructing rules; a PTE/IELTS essay structure or "describe image" template you can run automatically; a rehearsed interview story you can deliver without reassembling it; letter-sound blends and sight words for early literacy.

---

## 3. Building a chunked library (the multi-pass problem cycle)

- **Mechanism.** Repeated, spaced re-solving of the *same* key item, with sleep in the middle, both strengthens the pattern and builds the intuition that lets each completed step signal the next. A stock of such units is what the book credits for expert-feeling problem solving.
- **Learner procedure.** Explicit six-step recipe: (1) work a key problem fully on paper, no step-skipping, without consulting the solution unless truly stuck; (2) work it again, attending to the key moves; (3) take a break and do something unrelated; (4) before sleeping, work it once more, and if stuck, hold the problem in mind as you fall asleep; (5) next day, work it again — now faster — and concentrate the effort on the parts that are still hard; (6) move to a new key problem and repeat. If time-poor, apply this to a small number of high-value items rather than everything.
- **Category.** study-time practice (with scheduling structure).
- **Chapter.** 7.
- **Domain.** Domain-bound in form (assumes discrete problems with checkable solutions), general in logic. Equivalents: re-do the same IELTS writing task across four spaced passes; re-record the same interview answer over several days; re-work the same translation or cloze exercise; re-read and re-tell the same short passage on successive days for a young reader.

---

## 4. Illusions of competence — rereading, highlighting, glancing at solutions

- **Mechanism.** Fluency is mistaken for knowledge. Re-encountering material makes it feel easy to process, and the mind reads that ease as evidence of learning. Having the book, the worked solution, or a search engine open in front of you creates the impression that the content is also in your head. Highlighting adds a motor illusion — the hand moves, so something feels accomplished.
- **Learner procedure.** Treat three specific behaviours as red flags: passive rereading, heavy highlighting, and reading a worked solution and concluding you could reproduce it. Countermeasures the book prescribes: never highlight a passage you haven't first recalled from memory; cap marking at roughly one sentence per paragraph and prefer short synthesising notes in the margin; use provided solutions only to check your own completed attempt; and prove retention by generating the material with the source closed.
- **Category.** metacognitive check.
- **Chapter.** 4 (illusion mechanics), 18 (listed among bad-study behaviours).
- **Domain.** General. Equivalents: rereading a vocabulary list versus producing the words; reviewing a model IELTS essay versus writing your own to time; reading a "top interview answers" article versus saying yours aloud; a child following along in a familiar book versus decoding unaided.

---

## 5. Active recall / retrieval practice

- **Mechanism.** Generating material from memory strengthens and stabilises the underlying pattern far more than re-exposure does, and the effort of retrieval is itself what deepens the trace. The book presents recall as the highest-yield single study behaviour and as the best form of deliberate practice in study.
- **Learner procedure.** After reading a page or section, look away and reconstruct the main ideas — while also working to understand what you're recalling. Glance back, reread, try again. Do the same at chapter level. Written or spoken recall counts; the book pairs recall with brief self-generated questions about the material.
- **Category.** study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 4, reinforced throughout (every chapter ends with a recall prompt), and rule 1 of the closing good-study list.
- **Domain.** Fully general — arguably the book's most transferable technique. Direct analogues in every listed domain: recall vocabulary before checking, reconstruct an essay outline from memory, recall your STAR story beats unaided, ask a child to retell a story.

---

## 6. Recall in varied physical locations

- **Mechanism.** Two stated reasons. First, retrieving material in a place unlike where you studied forces a different perspective on it. Second, memory picks up incidental environmental cues (the chair, the music, the room), and if all your practice happens in one place, an unfamiliar exam room removes those supports. Practising across settings makes retrieval location-independent.
- **Learner procedure.** After recalling material where you studied, deliberately recall it again elsewhere — another room, outdoors, walking to class. Repeat across genuinely different environments.
- **Category.** study-time practice / memory-encoding.
- **Chapter.** 4, reused as a prompt in 5 and 11; rule 1 of the closing list.
- **Domain.** General. Directly relevant to any high-stakes test taken in an unfamiliar room (PTE/IELTS test centres, interview rooms). App implication: prompt recall sessions in changing contexts, and don't let all practice happen at one device in one place.

---

## 7. Interleaving versus blocked practice

- **Mechanism.** Practising one technique repeatedly within a session produces mimicry of the previous item rather than genuine selection; the book's image is learning carpentry with only a hammer, so everything looks like a nail. Mastery means choosing the right approach for an unfamiliar item, and only mixed practice trains that choice. Mixing feels harder in the moment but produces deeper learning.
- **Learner procedure.** Once you have the basic grip on a technique, stop drilling it and start mixing item types that demand different approaches. Concrete tactics given: skip around across chapters and materials when reviewing; use the more varied end-of-chapter problem sets; flip randomly through the book, pick an item, and try to solve it unaided; when you have several flash cards, run them together rather than one at a time; and deliberately ask yourself *why* a given item calls for one approach rather than another. The book notes textbooks are organised against interleaving — a section heading tells you the answer — so you must impose the mixing yourself.
- **Category.** study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 4 (introduced), 18 (rule 5).
- **Domain.** Domain-bound in its examples (problem types with distinct solution strategies) but the principle is general. Equivalents: mix tense/case exercises instead of drilling one form; rotate randomly across all PTE/IELTS task types in one session rather than a week of "describe image"; shuffle behavioural, technical, and situational interview questions; mix phonics patterns rather than a whole week on one.

---

## 8. Overlearning as a trap

- **Mechanism.** Defined precisely: continuing to practise *after* the criterion is already met, in the same session. The book concedes it has a place for building automaticity in motor performance (a tennis serve, a piano piece), but argues that in study it consumes session time that would be better spent on unlearned material and does not strengthen the long-term connections you actually want. It is also flagged as a form of illusion of competence — you keep practising what you can already do because succeeding feels good.
- **Learner procedure.** Once you can do something, stop and move on; return to it on a later day (i.e. convert extra repetition into spacing and interleaving rather than same-session repetition). Watch for the emotional tell: choosing easy, pleasant items.
- **Category.** metacognitive check / study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 4; echoed in 18's bad-study list.
- **Domain.** General. Equivalents: re-answering vocabulary you already know, re-writing the essay type you're best at, rehearsing your strongest interview story a tenth time, a child re-reading the book they've mastered.

---

## 9. Deliberate practice on the hardest parts

- **Mechanism.** Modelled on musicians and chess players: a skilled violinist doesn't replay the whole piece repeatedly, she isolates the passages where the fingers stumble. Similarly, what distinguishes chess grandmasters is time spent identifying and attacking their own weaknesses. Applied to study, the leverage is in the specific sub-steps that remain effortful.
- **Learner procedure.** On later passes through material, stop computing every routine step and concentrate on the specific portions that are still difficult or slow. Explicitly identify weaknesses rather than practising generally. Recall — not rereading — is named as the best form of this. Also: after every assignment and test, go over your errors, work out why each occurred, and rework the solution.
- **Category.** study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 7; error-review appears again in 18 (rule 5).
- **Domain.** Domain-bound in illustration, general in logic. Equivalents: isolate the sounds or grammatical forms you keep getting wrong; for PTE/IELTS, drill your specific weak band descriptor (e.g. cohesive devices, or pronunciation of a particular cluster) rather than whole mock tests; for interviews, rehearse the one question that unsettles you; for literacy, target the specific decoding pattern the child fails on. Strong hook for an app: error logging plus weakness-targeted re-queueing.

## 10. The Pomodoro technique

- **Mechanism.** Solves two problems at once. It removes the anxiety of an open-ended, unpleasant task by bounding the commitment to a short interval, and it supplies a reward at the end, which is what makes the behaviour habit-forming. The bounded interval also enforces the focused/diffuse alternation and lets you reach a state of absorbed, automatic work without needing to finish anything.
- **Learner procedure.** Silence phone and computer notifications; set a timer for twenty-five minutes; work with full attention on the task without breaking for messages or browsing; explicitly do not aim to finish anything — aim only to work. When the timer ends, take a real reward: browsing, phone, a snack, a walk. Rest length is scaled to circumstances (a generous half hour when deadlines are distant; a few minutes when under pressure). Several such intervals per day. Optionally identify in advance the single most important task of the day and spend intervals on it. The book also suggests alternating timed and untimed sessions, returning to the timer whenever focus lapses.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit.
- **Chapter.** 2 (introduced), 6 (developed), 18 (rule 8).
- **Domain.** Fully general and directly implementable in software (timer, notification suppression, session logging, reward prompt).

---

## 11. Process over product

- **Mechanism.** The pain that triggers avoidance is attached to the *outcome* (the finished assignment, the completed essay). Attention on the outcome produces the discomfort; attention on elapsed time and habitual action does not. The book adds a habit-system argument: the automatic, non-deliberative part of the brain can execute a process happily but cannot be enlisted to deliver a product.
- **Learner procedure.** Reframe commitments in units of time and routine rather than deliverables — commit to twenty minutes of work, not to finishing five problems. Judge the session on whether you put in calm effort for the interval, explicitly *not* on whether you finished or understood anything in that sitting.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit / metacognitive framing.
- **Chapter.** 6.
- **Domain.** Fully general. App implication: goals expressed as sessions and streaks, not as completion percentages; avoid outcome-framed nagging.

---

## 12. The habit loop and cue re-engineering

- **Mechanism.** Habits are described as four-part structures: a **cue** that launches the automatic response; the **routine** itself; the **reward** that sustains it; and the underlying **belief** that makes it feel immovable. Habit is framed as an energy-saving mode closely related to chunking — you stop consciously attending. Procrastination is a habit whose reward (relief, attention shifted to something pleasant) arrives almost instantly, which is why it entrenches easily. Willpower is treated as a scarce, expensive resource, so the strategy is to spend it at exactly one point rather than across the whole behaviour.
- **Learner procedure.** Four matched interventions:
  - *Cue* — identify what launches the avoidance. Cues fall into categories: place, time of day, emotional state, other people's behaviour, or a triggering event. Then either remove the cue (phone off, internet disconnected, phone left in the car or handed to someone else, relocate to a quiet library) or install a better one (start work immediately on arriving home, or right after a particular class).
  - *Routine* — this is the single pressure point where willpower is applied. Have a written plan and a substitute ritual in advance. Don't attempt a wholesale overhaul; overwrite parts. Expect the plan to need adjustment. Having eaten helps supply the energy for that initial push.
  - *Reward* — investigate what the old habit was paying you, then substitute a deliberate payoff: a small feeling of pride, a personal game or bet, a coffee, guilt-free browsing, a bigger reward for a bigger achievement. Rewards should be delayed until the task is done, and consciously savoured. Fixed-time rewards (lunch with a friend at noon, hard stop at five) act as mini-deadlines. Over time the work itself becomes rewarding.
  - *Belief* — treated as the decisive component for durability, sustained partly by joining a community of people who hold the belief you want.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit.
- **Chapter.** 6; the willpower argument in 5; wrap-up in 9.
- **Domain.** Fully general, and the richest source of app features: cue logging, distraction blocking, ritual definition, reward scheduling, streak/community elements.

## 13. Spaced repetition (and why cramming fails)

- **Mechanism.** Moving material from the four-slot working memory into long-term storage takes time, and the consolidation happens *between* sessions, not during them. The book's image is bricklaying: sessions lay bricks, and the intervening time lets the mortar set — cram everything at once and you get a heap rather than a wall. It also notes that an initial pattern needs strengthening within about a day or it fades, and that rereading only helps if enough time has passed for it to function as spacing.
- **Learner procedure.** Study any subject a little every day rather than in long blocks; spread repetitions of a given item across multiple days; review new material within a day of first meeting it. The book endorses spaced-repetition flash card software (Anki is named) on the grounds that the intervals are built in, and it suggests building an action plan so that the timely review actually happens rather than slipping.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit / memory-encoding.
- **Chapter.** 3; rule 4 and bad-study rule 4 in 18.
- **Domain.** Fully general; the book explicitly treats vocabulary as a spacing case. The most obviously implementable technique in the book — scheduling algorithm, next-day review nudge, daily-dose targets.

## 14. Vivid multisensory images

- **Mechanism.** Humans have disproportionately strong visual and spatial memory — the book's evolutionary framing is that our ancestors needed to remember routes and the location of food, not names and numbers. So encoding abstract material as a picture recruits a far more capable system than repetition does, and each additional sense engaged (sight, sound, touch, movement) adds another retrieval route, producing a denser web that survives exam stress better. Images also free working-memory capacity.
- **Learner procedure.** Convert what you want to remember into a concrete, striking, often absurd image — and then extend it beyond sight: hear it, feel it, add movement. Say the item and its meaning aloud to add an auditory route. The funnier and more evocative the image, the better it holds. A physical example given is handling anatomy models with eyes closed while naming each part aloud.
- **Category.** memory-encoding.
- **Chapter.** 10.
- **Domain.** General, and the book applies it explicitly to equations and symbols (e.g. reading a division slash as a playground slide). Equivalents: image-based vocabulary encoding in a new language; visualising an IELTS collocation in a scene; visual anchors for interview talking points; picture-and-action phonics for young readers.

---

## 15. The memory palace

- **Mechanism.** A specific application of the visuospatial advantage: a familiar layout you already know perfectly provides a free, ordered scaffold onto which arbitrary items can be pegged. The book frames it as a disciplined creative exercise that trains focus while simultaneously generating the wide, loose associations that make memories stick.
- **Learner procedure.** Pick a thoroughly familiar place — your home, your route to school, a favourite restaurant. Walk through it mentally in a fixed order and deposit one memorable image per location. Retrieve by walking the route again. Best suited to ordered lists of otherwise unrelated items (list of elements, sequence of steps, layers of a structure, points of a speech). It can be layered onto a first-letter mnemonic sentence when the sentence alone is hard to hold. It's slow the first few times and speeds up with practice; one cited study reported very high recall of a 40–50 item list after only one or two mental walks. Caveat given: because it occupies the spatial system, don't use it while driving.
- **Category.** memory-encoding.
- **Chapter.** 10.
- **Domain.** General, and explicitly recommended for speeches and presentations as well as academic lists. Equivalents: ordered vocabulary sets or irregular-verb groups; the fixed sequence of points in an IELTS Task 2 essay; the ordered beats of an interview answer or presentation.

---

## 16. Meaningful groupings, acronyms, mnemonic sentences, stories and songs

- **Mechanism.** Grouping and abbreviating compress material into fewer working-memory units, which is the same economy chunking provides. Stories and songs preferentially engage systems the brain retains easily. The book notes that repetition still matters — these devices firm the material up before it fades.
- **Learner procedure.** Take a list, extract first letters, and turn them into a word or image you can retrieve (the book's example turns four initials into a graham cracker), or build a memorable sentence from them. Wrap sequences into a short silly narrative. Set material to a tune. Combine with the memory palace when the mnemonic itself is slippery. **Important caveat the book raises via a chemistry professor:** a memory device is a retrieval handle, not understanding — learners sometimes mistake the mnemonic's logic for the real reason behind the grouping. Memorise material you already understand.
- **Category.** memory-encoding.
- **Chapter.** 11.
- **Domain.** General. The caveat matters most for exam prep: an IELTS template or a mnemonic for essay structure is a handle, not competence.

---

## 17. Handwriting rather than typing (and saying it aloud)

- **Mechanism.** Writing by hand is reported to get ideas into mind more readily than typing, plausibly via a motor component — the book calls it a kind of muscle memory and is candid that the research base here is thin. Speaking and writing both add extra encoding routes beyond thinking.
- **Learner procedure.** Write initial solutions, diagrams, and concept notes out by hand. For flash cards, handwrite the prompt on one side and the answer on the other (photograph the card afterwards if you want it in a phone app). When an equation or formula looks meaningless, write it out thoughtfully several times and watch meaning accrue. Read problems or formulas aloud. Keep handwritten solutions physically organised — taped into the relevant textbook pages, or in a binder — so pre-test review is fast. Explicit limit: writing something a hundred times is rote waste; the first few repetitions carry the value.
- **Category.** memory-encoding / study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 4 and 11; rule 5 in 18.
- **Domain.** Partly domain-bound — one stated advantage is purely practical, that mathematical symbols are faster by hand than by keyboard. The encoding claim is general. Equivalents: handwrite new vocabulary and example sentences; handwrite IELTS essay plans (also matching paper-based test conditions); handwrite interview story notes; handwriting is already central to primary literacy. Design tension for an app: the book's advice partly argues *against* pure on-screen input, favouring hybrid capture (write by hand, photograph into the app).

## 18. Sleep

- **Mechanism.** The most strongly stated physiological claim in the book. Being awake generates metabolic waste; during sleep cells shrink, inter-cellular space opens, and fluid flushes the waste out, which is offered as the explanation for muddy thinking when under-slept. Sleep also prunes trivial detail while strengthening what matters, rehearses the harder parts of what you've been learning, and — with the conscious prefrontal "you" offline — lets brain regions communicate more freely, which is why difficult problems resolve overnight.
- **Learner procedure.** Three distinct practices: (a) get adequate sleep, and treat sleep the night before a test as non-negotiable — the book states flatly that skipping it can negate every other preparation step; (b) prime the process by working the problem, or reviewing what you want to learn, immediately before sleeping, and again on waking — otherwise the brain works on whatever else is on your mind; (c) use naps deliberately as a diffuse-mode tool (the Edison/Dalí anecdote; a practical suggestion of roughly a twenty-one-minute alarm to avoid grogginess, with slow breathing to fall asleep). Some learners keep a notebook to capture half-formed thoughts on either side of sleep.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit.
- **Chapter.** 3 (mechanism), 7 (built into the chunk-building cycle), 17 (test-eve), 18 (bad-study rule 10).
- **Domain.** Fully general. Direct app features: pre-sleep review prompt, wake-up review prompt, sleep-before-test reminder in a countdown.

---

## 19. Physical exercise

- **Mechanism.** Regular exercise is credited with substantive improvement to memory and learning, via generation of new neurons in memory-related regions and new signalling pathways. Both aerobic and resistance work are said to have comparably strong effects, with subtly different molecular profiles. A separate, smaller claim: brief exertion acutely clears mental fog.
- **Learner procedure.** Exercise regularly as a baseline learning intervention, not just for health. Acutely: when recall feels foggy or you notice yourself rereading the same paragraph, do a short burst — sit-ups, push-ups, jumping jacks — then attempt recall. Also used as a way to start an avoided task (do something physical first, then begin) and as a diffuse-mode break activity.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit.
- **Chapter.** 11; break-time exertion prompt in 6.
- **Domain.** Fully general.

## 20. Explaining simply and teaching others

- **Mechanism.** Simplifying for someone without your background forces you to locate and hold the essential structure, which builds and clarifies your own understanding rather than merely displaying it. Speaking or writing the explanation, rather than merely thinking it, adds encoding effort and therefore depth.
- **Learner procedure.** Named as **explanatory questioning**: when stuck on a concept, ask how you would explain it so a ten-year-old could follow, and lean on a simple analogy. Do not leave the explanation internal — say it out loud or write it down. Separately: relate what you've been learning to a family member, friend, or classmate; this works even for advanced material, and the simplification is where the benefit lies.
- **Category.** study-time practice / metacognitive check.
- **Chapter.** 4 (recall prompt), 18 (rule 7).
- **Domain.** Fully general and one of the easiest to instrument (record a spoken explanation; write a simplified version; peer teaching). Equivalents: explain a grammar rule in your own words; explain an IELTS graph in plain speech before writing; explain your own project to a non-specialist as interview practice; a child explaining the story back.

## 21. Testing as a learning event, not just assessment

- **Mechanism.** The **testing effect**: being tested changes and adds to what you know and markedly improves retention, apparently by strengthening and stabilising the relevant neural patterns. The book stresses that the gain appears even when performance is poor and no feedback is given. A second mechanism concerns choking: panic is described as working memory filling to capacity with no room left for the pieces you need, and well-chunked material compresses that load, leaving capacity free.
- **Learner procedure.** Self-test constantly and on everything, rather than treating tests as endpoints — flash cards, closing the book and solving cold, quizzing with classmates, random problem selection. When self-testing during study, do seek feedback and check against solutions (unlike the bare testing effect, this improves the loop). Build in pre-test smoothing: quickly outline many solutions without grinding through the arithmetic. On multiple-choice, cover the options and try to answer from memory before reading them.
- **Category.** study-time practice / metacognitive check.
- **Chapter.** 7; opening claim of 17; rule 2 in 18.
- **Domain.** General in principle; the "solve it cold" and "outline without calculating" forms are problem-specific. Equivalents: self-quiz vocabulary in both directions; timed mini-tasks under exam conditions for PTE/IELTS; mock interviews; regular unaided reading checks for a child.

---

## 22. Hard-start–jump-to-easy (exam sequencing)

- **Mechanism.** Inverts the conventional advice to do easy items first. Hard items need the most time and also need the loose, wide-ranging mode — but that mode is unavailable precisely while you are straining at the item. Starting hard and then leaving loads the problem into background processing while you make visible progress elsewhere; the book's analogy is a chef managing several dishes. It also guarantees at least partial work on every item (useful where partial credit exists) and reduces fixation, since you revisit items from fresh angles. The conventional order creates the worst case: hardest items encountered when time is shortest and stress highest, producing paralysis.
- **Learner procedure.** Scan the paper. Begin with a hard item. The moment you notice yourself stuck for a minute or two, deliberately pull off it and move to something you can progress on. Cycle back later; expect the next step to be more apparent. Requires the self-discipline to abandon an item mid-thought. **Stated exceptions:** when a very hard item carries few marks, skip it; when the exam format forbids returning to earlier questions, don't use this — take a couple of belly breaths and do your best; and if you're underprepared, take the easy marks. Practise the technique on homework first if you're unsure of it.
- **Category.** test-day tactic.
- **Chapter.** 17.
- **Domain.** Domain-bound in its cleanest form — it assumes a paper of separable, revisitable, partially creditable problems. Equivalents: in IELTS Reading or Writing, start the harder passage/task then rotate; in an interview, note a difficult question and ask to return to it, or begin structuring rather than answering; largely inapplicable to strictly linear computer-adaptive formats (which the book itself flags), and to primary literacy.

## 23. Breathing and stress reframing

- **Mechanism.** Stress releases chemicals producing sweaty palms, racing heart, and a knotted stomach — but the book's claim is that performance depends on the *interpretation* of those sensations, not the sensations themselves. Recasting them as excitement rather than fear measurably improves performance. Deep abdominal breathing is said to deliver oxygen to key brain regions and signal safety, calming the system. Related: severe stress is described as degrading the focused mode's ability to make connections at all.
- **Learner procedure.** Reframing: consciously substitute an eagerness interpretation for a fear interpretation of your own physical symptoms. Breathing: relax the stomach, place a hand on it, draw a slow deep breath so the hand moves outward and the chest expands — belly, not upper chest — and breathe fully out. Crucially, **train this in advance**: a minute or two here and there in the weeks before the test, so the pattern is available under pressure. The book also endorses letting intrusive thoughts drift past rather than suppressing them, citing a mindfulness study, and notes that writing about test worries beforehand can help.
- **Category.** test-day tactic (with a rehearsal component before the day).
- **Chapter.** 17; attitude reframing also in 8.
- **Domain.** Fully general — applies identically to PTE/IELTS, interviews, and performance situations. App implication: schedule breathing rehearsal in the weeks before a test date, not just on the day.

---

## 24. Einstellung — breaking mental fixation

- **Mechanism.** Named for the German for "installation": an idea already in mind installs a roadblock that prevents a better one being found. The pinball model illustrates it — attention keeps bouncing in a well-worn region of thought while the needed pattern sits elsewhere. Eye-tracking research on chess players is cited: players who believe they are scanning for alternatives are in fact still fixated on their original candidate. The book adds that this bites hardest in science, where naive intuitions are often wrong, so learners must unlearn while learning.
- **Learner procedure.** Recognise that accepting the first idea that arrives can block the better one. Countermeasures: switch out of the narrow mode (break, sleep, a different task); use hard-start–jump-to-easy to see items from different angles at different times; use metaphor or analogy, which the book specifically credits with breaking fixation; ask someone else for a different framing or analogy — but wrestle with it yourself first, since prior struggle makes you receptive to the explanation; and use blinking / a deliberate shift of attention as a small reset before re-evaluating. Related test-day move: because your mind can convince you an answer is right when it isn't, blink, shift attention, then re-check with a big-picture sanity question — does this actually make sense?
- **Category.** metacognitive check (with study-time and test-day applications).
- **Chapter.** 1 (introduced), 3, 11 (metaphor as a way out), 17 (re-checking).
- **Domain.** General, though the "wrong initial intuition" framing is sharpest in science. Equivalents: an entrenched L1-interference error in language learning; misreading what an IELTS question is asking and answering the wrong prompt; committing to a misjudged reading of an interview question; a child locked onto a wrong decoding guess.

## 25. Metaphor and analogy for abstraction (and transfer)

- **Mechanism.** A metaphor connects new material to neural structures already in place, giving something to trace the new pattern over — hence faster comprehension. At a deeper level the book argues that a good chunk *is* an abstraction, and abstractions resonate across domains, which is what makes **transfer** — applying what you learned in one context somewhere else — possible. It also notes metaphor's role in escaping fixation, citing the fortress-attack story used to help students grasp multi-directional radiation therapy. Explicit caveat: all metaphors and scientific models eventually break down.
- **Learner procedure.** For any difficult concept, deliberately construct a metaphor or analogy, preferably visual and ideally a little silly (electric current as flowing water, voltage as pressure, a limit as a runner approaching a line in slow motion). Ask whether some process in a completely unrelated field resembles what you're studying. If nothing comes, pick up a pen and doodle for a minute or two. Revise or discard metaphors as your understanding matures. Practise transfer explicitly: take a concrete application, step back to sense the abstract idea underneath, then imagine a completely different use for it. The book also offers **equation poems** — writing several unfolding lines that surface the meaning beneath a formula — and "staging a play" with the elements of a concept in your mind's eye. Warning: the book notes that multitasking during learning shallows the encoding and specifically damages your ability to transfer.
- **Category.** memory-encoding / study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 11 (metaphor), 13 (abstraction as chunk), 14 (equation poems, transfer).
- **Domain.** The equation-poem device is squarely domain-bound to symbolic material. Metaphor and transfer generalise fully. Equivalents: a metaphor for a grammatical case system; for IELTS, recognising that a structural pattern learned on one essay type serves another; for interviews, abstracting one project experience so it answers differently framed questions; for literacy, concrete analogies for abstract print concepts.

## 26. Working with others to catch blind spots

- **Mechanism.** The narrow, confident mode of thinking produces errors you feel certain about; the book's blunt position is that self-deception is the easiest kind. Other people — particularly those willing to disagree — supply the different processing that surfaces those errors. Additional benefits claimed: practice thinking on your feet under pressure, verification that you actually understand what you're explaining, and career connections. Rechecking your own work is presented as a weaker version of the same effect, engaging slightly different processes for a broader view.
- **Learner procedure.** Study and work problems with peers who will push back. Attempt items individually first, then compare solutions — or work them out jointly. Participate actively (contribute, ask questions) rather than watching others solve things, which the book flags as actively harmful. Quiz one another. Consult instructors and teaching assistants when confused instead of avoiding them. Recheck your own work: it usually takes far less time to check than to solve. Treat criticism, given or received, as being about the material, not about you. Guardrail: if the group turns social before the work is done, it's wasting your time — find another group.
- **Category.** study-time practice / metacognitive check.
- **Chapter.** 16; bad-study rules 6 and 8 in 18; embedded throughout the test-prep checklist.
- **Domain.** Fully general — perhaps more valuable outside maths, since language production and interview performance cannot be self-marked reliably. Equivalents: conversation partners and peer correction; peer or examiner feedback on IELTS speaking and writing (where self-assessment is notoriously unreliable); mock interviews with a critical friend; paired reading.

## 27. Test preparation checklist

- **Mechanism.** Explicitly modelled on the checklists used by pilots and doctors: a structured pre-flight review that converts "how should I prepare?" from an open question into a set of verifiable behaviours. The book presents this (adapted from a memo by Richard Felder) as one of its most central items, and notes it is diagnostic — a poor grade with many "no" answers is unsurprising, whereas a poor grade with mostly "yes" answers means something else is wrong and warrants talking to an instructor or counsellor.
- **Learner procedure.** Twelve yes/no items, answered honestly and only "yes" if the behaviour was habitual rather than occasional. Homework items: made a serious effort to understand the text (hunting for worked examples that resemble the problem doesn't count); worked with classmates or at least compared solutions; attempted to outline every solution before working with others. Preparation items: participated actively in group discussion; consulted the instructor or TAs when stuck; understood *all* your submitted solutions; asked in class about solutions that weren't clear; worked through any study guide and convinced yourself you could do everything on it; practised outlining many solutions quickly without grinding the algebra; went through the guide and problems with classmates and quizzed each other; attended and asked questions at any review session. Final item, weighted above all others: got a reasonable night's sleep — if not, the other eleven may not matter. The prescription is simply to change your preparation until you can answer yes to most items; two or more "no" answers is a signal to change something.
- **Category.** metacognitive check / test-day preparation.
- **Chapter.** 17.
- **Domain.** Domain-bound as written (homework problems, solution outlines, study guides, algebra). The *pattern* — a pre-test behavioural audit — generalises cleanly and is very easy to implement as an app feature. Equivalents: a PTE/IELTS checklist (have you done full timed mocks under real conditions? had writing marked by someone competent? recorded and reviewed your own speaking? checked the format of every task type? slept?); an interview checklist (researched the company, prepared stories for each competency, rehearsed aloud, tested the video setup); a literacy checklist for a parent.

## 28. The good-versus-bad study habits checklist

- **Mechanism.** A closing pair of ten-item lists that consolidates the whole book into behaviours, with the bad list framed specifically around techniques that waste time *while feeling productive* — i.e. it's an illusion-of-competence audit.
- **Learner procedure.** *Good practices:* use recall (look away and reconstruct after reading; highlight almost nothing and never before recalling; recall in different places); self-test constantly, using flash cards; chunk problems so a whole solution comes to mind at once and you can do it cold, step by step; space repetition — a little every day in each subject; alternate techniques within practice and quiz yourself randomly across types, review errors and rework them, and handwrite cards; take breaks when frustrated so background processing can work; use explanatory questioning and simple analogies, spoken or written rather than merely thought; focus in twenty-five-minute timed blocks with notifications off and a small reward after, in habitual times and places; do the hardest thing first thing in the day while fresh ("eat your frogs first"); and use mental contrasting — hold where you started against the future your studying leads to, with a visible reminder in your workspace for low-motivation moments.
  *Bad practices:* passive rereading; letting highlighting substitute for encoding; glancing at a solution and assuming you could produce it; leaving study to the last minute; repeatedly solving item types you already handle; letting group study drift into socialising; skipping the textbook before attempting problems (with a preliminary skim recommended); not asking instructors or classmates to clear up confusion; expecting deep learning while constantly interrupted; and not sleeping enough — stated in the strongest terms for the night before a test.
- **Category.** spans all categories; functions as a metacognitive self-audit.
- **Chapter.** 18.
- **Domain.** Mostly general; a few items (chunk your problems, alternate problem-solving techniques, read the textbook before doing problems) presuppose problem-set courses. An adapted checklist per domain is a natural app feature.

## 29. Planning: the planner-journal, evening task list, frogs first, and a quitting time

- **Mechanism.** Written planning off-loads tracking, exposes where time actually goes, and — for the evening list specifically — gives the loose background mode overnight to start working on the next day's goals. Small, finished, crossed-off items supply the frequent rewards the habit system needs. A defined stopping time matters because guilt-free rest is what makes the diffuse mode available and the whole system sustainable; the book treats scheduling leisure as one of the most important anti-procrastination moves, not a luxury.
- **Learner procedure.** Keep a planner-journal and log outcomes so you can see what works. Write weekly goals at the start of the week; each evening write five to ten small, reasonable daily tasks derived from them; break any daunting task into three smaller subtasks. Cross items off and deliberately savour each one. Do a weekly review for the bigger picture. Tackle the most disliked, most important task first in the day. Set a daily quitting time and actually stop, without guilt. Track your activities in detail for a week or two if you don't know where your problem areas are. Commit to fixed daily routines. Delay rewards until tasks are done. Have a backup plan for the days you procrastinate anyway. Choose the environment that suits you (some need a busy café, some a silent library). Resist blaming external factors for everything. Related: mental contrasting — hold your worst past or present circumstances against the future you're working toward.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit.
- **Chapter.** 8, consolidated in 9; rules 9–10 in 18.
- **Domain.** Fully general and highly implementable (evening planning prompt, weekly review, task decomposition, completion savouring, hard-stop reminder, activity logging).

## 30. Distraction control / not multitasking

- **Mechanism.** The book's image is repeatedly pulling up a seedling: each attention switch tears out tiny neural roots before they can establish. Consequences claimed: shallower connections, faster fatigue (each switch costs energy), more errors, poorer recall, and specifically reduced ability to transfer what you learn. Chunk formation is said to be impossible without undistracted attention. Students who multitask while studying are reported to get consistently lower grades.
- **Learner procedure.** Turn off notifications on phone and computer; disable sound alerts; put the phone in another room, in the car, on airplane mode, or in someone else's keeping (which doubles as a public commitment); disconnect internet access for the duration of a focused block; choose a low-interruption setting such as a quiet library. Let intruding thoughts drift past rather than fighting them.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit / study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 6 and 8; bad-study rule 9 in 18.
- **Domain.** Fully general. Direct app features: focus mode, notification suppression, block-list, session integrity tracking.

---

## 31. Learning independently and seeking out mentors

- **Mechanism.** Working through material yourself, beyond a single teacher or textbook, reveals that any one presentation is a partial view, and builds independent thinking — which the book connects to handling unexpected exam questions. Persistence is asserted to matter more than raw speed, and learners who grasp things slowly are described as often wrestling with genuinely fundamental issues that faster students skate over, arriving at deeper understanding.
- **Learner procedure.** Deliberately go beyond the assigned text — other books, other videos, other explanations of the same concept. Occasionally reach out to people you admire and introduce yourself; a single sentence from a good mentor can redirect you. Use teachers' and mentors' time sparingly. Expect some people to criticise or undercut you and handle it without drama.
- **Category.** scheduling-and-habit / study-time practice.
- **Chapter.** 15.
- **Domain.** Fully general; if anything more powerful in language learning and exam prep, where abundant alternative material exists.

# What the book itself treats as central versus peripheral

## Framed as most important

1. **Active recall / retrieval practice over rereading.** This gets the most repetition and the most emphasis of anything in the book. It is embedded structurally — every chapter closes with a recall prompt — and it is rule 1 of the closing good-study list. The book also calls it the best form of deliberate practice in study, making it the mechanism behind several other techniques rather than a technique among many.

2. **Chunking, including deliberate practice on the hard parts.** The organising theoretical concept. Two full chapters are devoted to it, it is invoked to explain habit, expertise, transfer, choking, creativity, and abstraction, and the multi-pass problem-building cycle is the book's most detailed procedural recipe.

3. **The focused/diffuse alternation.** The book's foundational model, introduced first and used to justify breaks, spacing, sleep, the Pomodoro, the hard-start exam strategy, and escaping fixation. Almost everything else in the book is downstream of it.

4. **Spacing over cramming.** Presented as a non-negotiable consequence of how consolidation works, with the brick-and-mortar image returned to repeatedly.

5. **Sleep.** The most emphatic single claim in the book — stated in capitals in the bad-study list and given the deciding position in the test-preparation checklist, on the grounds that without it the rest of your preparation may not count.

6. **Recognising illusions of competence.** Framed as the failure mode the book exists to expose; it is what makes the whole bad-study list cohere, and it is the reason recall is preferred to rereading.

7. **Procrastination control via the habit loop and the Pomodoro.** Given more page count than any other practical topic — four chapters plus a consolidated cross-chapter summary — on the explicit grounds that maths and science require daily accumulation that procrastination makes impossible. The book states directly that procrastination is important enough to warrant that treatment.

8. **The two checklists (test preparation, and the ten good/ten bad rules).** The book calls the test-preparation checklist one of the central features of both its test-taking chapter and the book as a whole, and the ten-rules pair is the closing distillation.

## Framed as significant but secondary

- **Interleaving and the warning against overlearning** — clearly argued and given its own section plus a closing rule, but treated as a refinement of practice design rather than a foundation.
- **Working with others and rechecking work** — a full chapter, but positioned as error insurance on top of solid individual preparation, not a substitute for it.
- **Metaphor and analogy** — endorsed strongly for comprehension, transfer, and breaking fixation, but with the explicit caveat that all metaphors break down and can be mistaken for understanding.
- **Handwriting over typing** — recommended and repeated, but the book is openly candid that the supporting research is thin.
- **Explaining simply / teaching others** — appears as a closing rule and several recall prompts; less developed than its prominence in later learning-science writing.
- **Test-day tactics (hard-start–jump-to-easy, breathing, reframing)** — genuinely novel contributions of the book, but hedged with explicit exceptions, and secondary to the claim that preparation is what determines outcomes.

## Framed as minor tips

- The **memory palace** and elaborate visual-image mnemonics — an engaging chapter, but presented mainly for ordered lists of otherwise unrelated items, and accompanied by the caution that a mnemonic is not knowledge. Not integrated into the book's core model of understanding.
- **Meaningful groupings, acronyms, mnemonic sentences, songs.**
- **Equation poems** and staging concepts as a play in the mind's eye — offered as imaginative exercises for one chapter.
- **Mental contrasting.**
- Small operational tricks: having food in your stomach before a hard start; blinking as a reset; marking your reading target with a sticky note for visible progress; naming your Pomodoro aloud so family leave you alone; specific nap length; the named apps and websites (a list the book itself acknowledges will date).
- **Exercise** — the mechanism claim is strong, but it occupies a short section and is positioned as general brain maintenance rather than a study technique.
